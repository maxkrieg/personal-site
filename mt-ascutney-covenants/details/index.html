History of the mountain

The rules hand their powers to the resort company "and any successors or assigns." The mountain moved on. The powers stayed behind.

  1. 1970
    Sky Hawk lots soldMt. Ascutney Ski Area, Inc. sells lots under its own covenants.
  2. 1984
    Rules rewrittenMt. Ascutney Corp. replaces the old covenants to support a hotel, condo, and health-club expansion.
  3. 1989
    Association incorporatedOwners form Mt. Ascutney Property Owners Association, Inc. as a separate nonprofit, five years after the rules.
  4. 1990
    First bankruptcyThe resort reopens in 1993 under new owners.
  5. 2010
    Foreclosure and closingMFW Associates, an investment firm, buys the resort's loan and forecloses. The resort closes for good.
  6. 2013
    AuctionMFW takes the resort's assets as the only bidder.
  7. 2015
    The town buys the mountainVoters approve it 254 to 79. Ascutney Outdoors forms to run it, and the land is conserved forever.
  8. Today
    The powers sit with MFWIn 2022 it was MFW, not the association, that reviewed and released the buy-first right on a home sale here.

Who looks after the mountain now

The townOwns the land469 acres added to the town forest in 2015.
Ascutney OutdoorsRuns itA volunteer nonprofit under a town lease.
Upper Valley Land TrustProtects itA conservation easement over 1,582 acres.

None of them has any role under the 1984 rules. The town also runs the sewer and water systems the rules tie to the resort, and maintains the roads.

The rules, word for word

The 1984 protective covenants are a 26-page document the old ski resort company recorded in the town land records. They apply to every property they cover, whether or not the owner has read them. Wherever they say "Ascutney," they mean the resort company or whoever inherited its rights, which today is MFW Associates. You can read the full document (PDF, 26 pages).

  • Your trees and your yard

    Evergreen trees over two (2) inches in diameter and deciduous trees over three (3) inches in diameter… may not be cut for any purpose whatsoever without the express prior written approval of AscutneyArticle IV.C.10

    Today: cutting almost any tree on your own lot needs the developer's written approval. A pine about as thick as a broom handle is covered.

    For example, a dead spruce leaning toward your roof is technically a violation to remove without written sign-off, and any neighbor could sue over it.

    Without the rules, you could clear a hazard tree, open a view, or make room for a garden on your own judgment.

  • Sheds, decks, additions, and signs

    No building, sign, facility or structure shall be constructed, erected, or excavated upon any property or lot except in accord with plans and specifications which have been approved in writing by Ascutney…Article V.D

    Today: any structure needs plans approved in writing by the developer, on top of town permits. Even a garden shed or a mailbox sign needs a private approval from a company that no longer runs anything here.

    Without the rules, you'd need town and state permits only, like everywhere else in West Windsor.

  • Backyard chickens

    No livestock or poultry of any kind shall be raised, bred or kept on any lotArticle V.E

    Today: banned outright, no matter how quiet or well kept.

    Without the rules, chickens would be allowed within the town's poultry ordinance, which already handles noise complaints.

  • Dues

    …the obligation to pay dues and costs of operating the Association, including costs of maintaining the Common AreasArticle VIII

    Today: required dues to an association that doesn't send owners a budget, and possible claims for back dues you were never billed for.

    Dues were meant to maintain shared land. The town maintains the roads and runs the sewer and water systems. Owners only see what dues pay for if they ask for the association's records.

    Without the rules, dues would be optional, more like a donation to a neighborhood group you choose to support. The town already plows and maintains the roads.

  • If you ever sell

    Such written notice shall constitute an offer to sell or lease the same premises to Ascutney upon the same terms contained in the notice.Article VII(b)

    Today: the developer's successor gets the first chance to buy your home, so every sale needs its waiver.

    When you accept an offer, you must mail the terms to the developer's successor, which has 10 days to buy your home at that price instead. It also covers leases longer than three years. In a 2022 sale here, the seller paid MFW Associates a "waiver review fee."

    Without the rules, it's a normal sale, with no notice or waiver.

  • Neighbor lawsuits

    The waiver by Ascutney or by any lot owner of any particular covenant… shall not be construed as a waiver of the same or a similar covenant… on any other occasion.Article IX

    Today: any owner can sue another over a technical violation, even an old one everyone ignored.

    Years of everyone ignoring a rule don't protect you. Each owner also agrees to share the cost of enforcement.

    Without the rules, real problems go through the town, with a hearing and a public record.

What the documents show

We read the full 1984 rules, a 2022 closing file, and the association's own notices.

  • Unconfirmed

    The rules admit nobody was sure what land they covered

    The 1984 document says earlier maps were never recorded, and it relied on a new map that should have been filed with the town.

    …there is confusion and inconsistency as to what property may be subject to said Protective CovenantsPreamble

    The same preamble admits the company wasn't sure its earlier, 1965 rules covered the condos or the Sky Hawk and Mountain Village lots at all. The 1984 rules were written to bring those properties in.

    …the Ascutney Mountainside Condominiums, Ascutney Mountain Village Condominiums and the Sky Hawk and Mountain Village lots are purportedly not subject to the Protective Covenants, although other areas of Mt. Ascutney Ski Area are subject to said covenantsPreamble

    Why it matters: if the 1984 map (Exhibit A) was never recorded, or your lot isn't on it, the rules may not cover your property at all.

    For example: the deed description for one Sky Hawk lot, carried forward through sales in 2004, 2010, and 2022, mentions only the covenants in its original 1970 deed. It never mentions the 1984 rules.

  • Unconfirmed

    They only took effect with a signed certification

    The rules required the company's Secretary to certify that every owner got notice and most approved. One may have been recorded, but it isn't in the copy that comes with closing documents.

    This amended Declaration shall become effective upon the recording thereof… together with a Certification by the SecretaryArticle XIV

    Why it matters: if that certification was never recorded, the 1984 rules may never have taken effect.

  • An investment firm still acts as the developer

    The seller's settlement statement for a 2022 home sale here lists a "Waiver review fee to MFW Associates, LLC" and a fee to record a release of MFW's right of first refusal.

    …all lots or condominium unit heretofore or subsequently conveyed by AscutneyArticle VII

    Why it matters: "Ascutney" means Mt. Ascutney Corp. Many Sky Hawk lots were sold around 1970 by an earlier company, so the buy-first right may never have applied to them.

  • Unconfirmed

    The rules never set up the association

    The rules only say owners will join "an Association of Owners." Today's association was incorporated separately in 1989, five years later.

    Ascutney expressly reserves the right to convey this right of first refusal to an association of ownersArticle VII(f)

    Why it matters: the rules let the developer hand its powers to an association. We found no sign that happened: in 2022, MFW still reviewed the buy-first waiver itself. The association may have bylaws, but owners aren't sent them, so it's unclear what powers it claims.

  • Whole sections describe a business that no longer exists

    The rules require hooking up to the resort's sewer system, let the developer charge road fees "including therein a reasonable profit," and promise ski access at regular rates. Today the town runs the sewer and water systems and maintains the roads, and there's no resort to ski at.

What the law says

Some of these rules are already cancelled by law. Others face real legal doubts.

  • Already cancelled

    Satellite dishes

    The rules ban dishes without approval. The FCC bars private rules, including approval requirements, that get in the way of a dish about 3 feet across or smaller on property you control. The FCC has struck down homeowner-association rules like ours.

    47 C.F.R. § 1.4000, the Over-the-Air Reception Devices Rule

  • Already cancelled

    Solar panels and clotheslines

    Vermont says no covenant can stop you from installing solar panels or a clothesline. An approving body can't say no. It can only pick where on a south-facing roof the panels go, and only if that doesn't hurt their output. Whoever loses a lawsuit over it pays the other side's legal fees. Since July 2026 the law also covers plug-in solar.

    27 V.S.A. § 544

  • Unconfirmed

    Rules can't be added after a lot is sold

    Vermont's Supreme Court has said neighborhood-wide rules must be in place before the first lot is sold, or agreed to by every owner. Sky Hawk lots were sold starting around 1970. The 1984 rules came about 15 years later and relied on a majority vote, not every owner's agreement.

    The complication: the original 1969 Sky Hawk covenants let the company amend them when it "shall deem such notification to be in the best interest of the development" (Preamble), so Sky Hawk buyers arguably agreed to future changes. The 1984 rules still only took effect with notice to every owner and approval by a majority, certified by the company's Secretary (Article XIV), and that certification isn't in the copy that comes with closing documents.

    Patch v. Springfield School District, No. 2008-366 (Vt. Nov. 20, 2009). The court quoted the rule that such restrictions can never be imposed "retroactively."

  • Unsettled in Vermont

    Rules meant for a resort that no longer exists

    Courts often stop enforcing rules whose purpose can no longer be met, or a right that never expires.

    Changed conditions: the trial court in Patch found covenants unenforceable for changed circumstances. The Supreme Court decided the case on other grounds. Here the purpose was a resort expansion on land that is now town-owned and conserved forever.

    A right with no end date: the developer's right to buy first lasts forever and passes to anyone who inherits it. Vermont hasn't ruled on such a right. It applies a "wait and see" rule against perpetuities (27 V.S.A. § 501; Colby v. Colby, 1991). Other states limit or strike these rights down.

    The 40-year title law: Vermont can clear claims older than 40 years from a property's title (27 V.S.A. §§ 601–606). The 1984 rules turned 40 in 2024, though later deeds that mention them may keep them alive.

  • Unconfirmed

    Your right to see the association's books

    Vermont law likely lets owners inspect the association's financial records and minutes, and requires a properly noticed annual meeting. Send a written request naming the records you want; the association has five days to make them available. Annual meetings need 10 to 60 days' notice and an agenda that lists any budget changes. This applies if the development counts as a "common interest community."

    27A V.S.A. §§ 1-204, 3-108, 3-118

The owners' association

Mt. Ascutney Property Owners Association, Inc. is a Vermont nonprofit that sends dues requests. It went about three years without an owners' meeting until October 2026, though Vermont law requires one every year. The 1984 rules are what require owners to join an association and pay dues.

Incorporated as a Vermont nonprofit, five years after the rules.
Apr. 2016
Registration terminated by the state for missed filings. Reinstated within days.
2025
When it acted against a neighborhood problem, it used a town ordinance, not the covenants.
Dec. 2025
Cancelled its annual meeting a week beforehand.
Apr. 2026
Dissolved by the state for missed filings. Reinstated within days.
Oct. 2026
Held its first owners' meeting in about three years. The roughly 30 owners there agreed to start looking into what it would take to end the covenants.
Today
In good standing. Owners get a short letter or email asking for dues, without a budget or bylaws. Its spending records are available if you ask.

It does real community work. It has a seat on the board of Ascutney Outdoors and on the town's Utility Advisory Committee, and it sponsored the 2025 Brownsville July 4th events. A voluntary neighbors' group could keep doing all of it without recorded covenants or mandatory dues.

Ending the covenants wouldn't shut the association down. It's a separate nonprofit corporation, and only its own board and members can dissolve it. Your right to see its books

Questions

Won't we lose protections for the neighborhood?

No. Town zoning, the noise and poultry ordinances, building and septic permits, and Act 250 all keep applying. When the association acted against a neighborhood problem in 2025, it used a town ordinance anyway. Neighbors who want extra rules can sign a new, voluntary agreement among themselves.

Would ending the covenants shut down the owners' association?

No. The association is a separate nonprofit corporation, and only its own board and members can dissolve it. Ending the covenants removes the requirement that every owner join and pay dues. The association can still hold meetings, keep its community seats, and keep sponsoring events, with dues as an optional contribution from anyone who wants to support it.

Doesn't the association speak for us?

It has a seat on the board of Ascutney Outdoors and on the town's Utility Advisory Committee, and it sponsored the 2025 Brownsville July 4th events. That's real community work. A voluntary neighbors' group could keep doing all of it without recorded covenants or mandatory dues.

Why not just fix the bad parts?

Owners can't. Only the developer's successor can propose changes (Article VI.B), and today that's MFW Associates.

Do the covenants ever expire?

Not on their own. They ran for 25 years and now renew automatically every 10 years unless a majority of lot owners vote to end them (Article XIII). The next renewal is in May 2029. At the association's October 2026 meeting, the owners there agreed to start looking into what ending them would take.

Does this apply to me?

Probably, if you own in Sky Hawk Village, Mountain Village, or the Ascutney Mountainside or Mountain Village condominiums. Your deed or title insurance policy will mention "protective covenants" from Mt. Ascutney Ski Area, Inc. or Mt. Ascutney Corp.
