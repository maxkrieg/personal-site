Mt. Ascutney · West Windsor, Vermont · For lot and condo owners

A proposal to end the 1984 Mt. Ascutney covenants

They were written for a ski resort that closed in 2010, yet they still decide what we can build, cut, and keep on our own land. If more than half of owners sign, they end.

  • 1984Rules written for a resort expansion
  • 2010The ski resort closed for good
  • 1,582Acres of the mountain now protected forever

New to this? Start here.

If you own a home, lot, or condo near the base of Mt. Ascutney, your property likely comes with two things many owners have never heard of.

On this page, "the rules" The 1984 protective covenants

A 26-page document the old ski resort company recorded in the town land records. It sets rules for every property it covers, whether or not you've ever read it.

On this page, "the association" Mt. Ascutney Property Owners Association, Inc.

A nonprofit formed in 1989. It sends dues letters and holds annual meetings. The 1984 rules are what require owners to join an association and pay dues.

Does this apply to you? Probably, if you're in Sky Hawk Village, Mountain Village, or the Ascutney Mountainside or Mountain Village condominiums. Your deed or title insurance policy will mention "protective covenants" from Mt. Ascutney Ski Area, Inc. or Mt. Ascutney Corp.

The one-minute version

  • In 1984 the resort company recorded rules on every lot and condo here. They decide what you can build, which trees you can cut, and whether you can keep chickens.
  • The resort went bankrupt and closed in 2010. The town now owns the mountain, and the land is protected forever. The hotel and condo plans those rules served can never happen.
  • The owners' association that collects dues hasn't published a budget and cancelled its own annual meeting last year.
  • The resort's powers over our homes now sit with an out-of-state investment firm that took over the resort in foreclosure.

What changes for you

These rules apply to your home today. Open any item to read the actual wording.

  • Your trees and your yard

    TodayCutting almost any tree on your own lot needs the developer's written approval.
    AfterClear a hazard tree, open a view, or make room for a garden on your own judgment.
    Read the rule
    The rule saysEvergreen trees over two (2) inches in diameter and deciduous trees over three (3) inches in diameter… may not be cut for any purpose whatsoever without the express prior written approval of AscutneyArticle IV.C.10
    In plain English

    A pine about as thick as a broom handle is covered. "Ascutney" means the resort company or whoever inherited its rights.

    For example

    A dead spruce leaning toward your roof is technically a violation to remove without written sign-off, and any neighbor could sue over it.

  • Sheds, decks, additions, and signs

    TodayAny structure needs plans approved in writing by the developer, on top of town permits.
    AfterTown and state permits only, like everywhere else in West Windsor.
    Read the rule
    The rule saysNo building, sign, facility or structure shall be constructed, erected, or excavated upon any property or lot except in accord with plans and specifications which have been approved in writing by AscutneyArticle V.D
    In plain English

    Even a garden shed or a mailbox sign needs a private approval from a company that no longer runs anything here.

  • Backyard chickens

    TodayBanned outright, no matter how quiet or well kept.
    AfterAllowed within the town's poultry ordinance, which already handles noise complaints.
    Read the rule
    The rule saysNo livestock or poultry of any kind shall be raised, bred or kept on any lotArticle V.E

  • Dues

    TodayDues to an association that hasn't published a budget, and possible claims for back dues you were never billed for.
    AfterNo dues. The town already plows and maintains the roads.
    Read the rule
    The rule says…the obligation to pay dues and costs of operating the Association, including costs of maintaining the Common AreasArticle VIII
    In plain English

    Dues were meant to maintain shared land. The town maintains the roads and runs the sewer and water systems, and the association hasn't published what dues pay for.

  • Neighbor disputes

    TodayAny owner can sue another over a technical violation, even an old one everyone ignored.
    AfterReal problems go through the town, with a hearing and a public record.
    Read the rule
    The rule saysThe waiver by Ascutney or by any lot owner of any particular covenant… shall not be construed as a waiver of the same or a similar covenant… on any other occasion.Article IX
    In plain English

    Years of everyone ignoring a rule don't protect you. Each owner also agrees to share the cost of enforcement.

  • Protecting the neighborhood

    TodayThe rules only work if someone enforces them. When the association acted in 2025, it used a town ordinance anyway.
    AfterTown zoning, the noise and poultry ordinances, septic and building permits, and Act 250 all stay in force.

  • If you ever sell

    TodayThe developer's successor gets first chance to buy your home, so every sale needs its waiver.
    AfterA normal sale, with no notice or waiver.
    Read the rule
    The rule saysSuch written notice shall constitute an offer to sell or lease the same premises to Ascutney upon the same terms contained in the notice.Article VII(b)
    In plain English

    When you accept an offer, you must mail the terms to the developer's successor, which has 10 days to buy your home at that price instead. It also covers leases longer than three years.

What happened to the mountain

The rules hand their powers to the resort company "and any successors or assigns." The mountain moved on. The powers stayed behind.

  1. 1970
    Sky Hawk lots soldMt. Ascutney Ski Area, Inc. sells lots under its own covenants.
  2. 1984
    Rules rewrittenMt. Ascutney Corp. replaces the old covenants to support a hotel, condo, and health-club expansion.
  3. 1989
    Association incorporatedOwners form Mt. Ascutney Property Owners Association, Inc. as a separate nonprofit, five years after the rules.
  4. 1990
    First bankruptcyThe resort reopens in 1993 under new owners.
  5. 2010
    Foreclosure and closingMFW Associates, an investment firm, buys the resort's loan and forecloses. The resort closes for good.
  6. 2013
    AuctionMFW takes the resort's assets as the only bidder.
  7. 2015
    The town buys the mountainVoters approve it 254 to 79. Ascutney Outdoors forms to run it, and the land is conserved forever.
  8. Today
    The powers sit with MFWIn 2022 it was MFW, not the association, that reviewed and released the buy-first right on a home sale here.

What we found in the documents

We read the full 1984 rules, a recent closing file, and the association's own notices.

  • The rules admit nobody was sure what land they covered

    The 1984 document says earlier maps were never recorded, and it relied on a new map that should have been filed with the town.

    See the wording
    The rules say…there is confusion and inconsistency as to what property may be subject to said Protective CovenantsPreamble
    Why it matters

    If the 1984 map (Exhibit A) was never recorded, or your lot isn't on it, the rules may not cover your property at all. The town clerk's map book will show it.

    For example

    The deed description for one Sky Hawk lot, carried forward through sales in 2004, 2010, and 2022, mentions only the covenants in its original 1970 deed. It never mentions the 1984 rules.

  • They only took effect with a signed certification

    The rules required the company's Secretary to certify that every owner got notice and most approved. The copy owners receive doesn't include one. The town land records will show whether it was ever filed.

    See the wording
    The rules sayThis amended Declaration shall become effective upon the recording thereof… together with a Certification by the SecretaryArticle XIV
    Why it matters

    If that certification was never recorded, the 1984 rules may never have taken effect.

  • The association was promised, never set up by the rules

    The rules only say owners will join "an Association of Owners." Today's association was incorporated separately in 1989, five years later. No bylaws come with the copy owners receive, and we couldn't find any published. In 2022, MFW, not the association, handled the buy-first waiver.

    See the wording
    The rules sayAscutney expressly reserves the right to convey this right of first refusal to an association of ownersArticle VII(f)
    Why it matters

    The rules let the developer hand its powers to the association. We found no sign that happened: MFW still reviewed the 2022 waiver itself. A search of the town land records would settle it. Mt. Ascutney Property Owners Association, Inc. is a Vermont nonprofit formed on August 25, 1989, and is currently in good standing. The state terminated its registration in April 2016 and dissolved it in April 2026 for missed filings; both times it was reinstated within days. It does some community work, such as a seat on the town's utility committee, but it cancelled its December 2025 annual meeting a week beforehand.

  • Whole sections describe a business that no longer exists

    The rules require hooking up to the resort's sewer system, let the developer charge road fees "including therein a reasonable profit," and promise ski access at regular rates. Today the town runs the sewer and water systems and maintains the roads.

  • An investment firm still acts as the developer

    A 2022 home sale here paid MFW Associates a "waiver review fee." Its right may not even cover many of our lots.

    See the details
    What the closing file shows

    The seller's settlement statement lists a "Waiver review fee to MFW Associates, LLC" and a fee to record a release of MFW's right of first refusal.

    The rules say…all lots or condominium unit heretofore or subsequently conveyed by AscutneyArticle VII
    Why it matters

    "Ascutney" means Mt. Ascutney Corp. Many Sky Hawk lots were sold around 1970 by an earlier company, so the right may never have applied to them.

What the law says

Some of these rules are already cancelled by law. Others face real legal doubts.

  • Already cancelled

    Satellite dishes

    The rules ban dishes without approval. Federal law overrides that.

    How it works
    In plain English

    The FCC bars private rules, including approval requirements, that get in the way of a dish about 3 feet across or smaller on property you control. The FCC has struck down homeowner-association rules like ours.

    The law47 C.F.R. § 1.4000, the Over-the-Air Reception Devices Rule
  • Already cancelled

    Solar panels and clotheslines

    Vermont says no covenant can stop you from installing solar panels or a clothesline.

    How it works
    In plain English

    An approving body can't say no. It can only pick where on a south-facing roof the panels go, and only if that doesn't hurt their output. Whoever loses a lawsuit over it pays the other side's legal fees. Since July 2026 the law also covers plug-in solar.

    The law27 V.S.A. § 544
  • Likely applies

    Your right to see the association's books

    Vermont law likely lets owners inspect the association's financial records and minutes, and requires a properly noticed annual meeting.

    How it works
    In plain English

    Send a written request naming the records you want. The association has five days to make them available. Annual meetings need 10 to 60 days' notice and an agenda that lists any budget changes. This applies if the development counts as a "common interest community," which an attorney should confirm.

    The law27A V.S.A. §§ 1-204, 3-108, 3-118
  • Strong for owners

    Rules can't be added after a lot is sold

    Vermont's Supreme Court has said neighborhood-wide rules must be in place before the first lot is sold, or agreed to by every owner.

    How it works
    In plain English

    Sky Hawk lots were sold starting around 1970. The 1984 rules came about 15 years later and relied on a majority vote, not every owner's agreement.

    The casePatch v. Springfield School District, No. 2008-366 (Vt. Nov. 20, 2009)The court quoted the rule that such restrictions can never be imposed "retroactively."
  • Unsettled in Vermont

    Rules meant for a resort that no longer exists

    Courts often stop enforcing rules whose purpose can no longer be met, or a right that never expires.

    How it works
    Changed conditions

    The trial court in Patch found covenants unenforceable for changed circumstances. The Supreme Court decided the case on other grounds. Here the purpose was a resort expansion on land that is now town-owned and conserved forever.

    A right with no end date

    The developer's right to buy first lasts forever and passes to anyone who inherits it. Vermont hasn't ruled on such a right. It applies a "wait and see" rule against perpetuities (27 V.S.A. § 501; Colby v. Colby, 1991). Other states limit or strike these rights down.

    The 40-year title law

    Vermont can clear claims older than 40 years from a property's title (27 V.S.A. §§ 601–606). The 1984 rules turned 40 in 2024, though later deeds that mention them may keep them alive.

How we end them

A majority of owners can end the rules by signing, with no meeting needed. If we do nothing, they renew automatically in May 2029 for 10 more years.

What we're asking: sign a short written form to end the rules. It costs you nothing. Roads, utilities, and town zoning all stay the same. You don't need to attend a meeting, and you can sign even if you never paid dues.
  1. Find every ownerEach owner gets one vote, however many lots they own, whether or not they paid dues.
  2. Sign the formEach owner signs a one-page written consent. We need more than half.
  3. File it with the townAn attorney records it in the West Windsor land records.
Read the rules on voting and ending
The rules sayThis Declaration shall continue for twenty-five years… and thereafter it shall continue for successive ten year periods unless and until it is terminated by the vote of a majority of the lot owners.Article XIII
The rules saySuch votes may be taken in or out of meeting and must be evidenced by a written instrument signed by the lot owner of recordArticle XI
In plain English

Signed paper forms count as votes. Roads, rights-of-way, and utility lines stay in place after the rules end.

Your signature counts as much as anyone's.

Every owner has one vote. The town keeps maintaining the roads, and town zoning keeps protecting the neighborhood.

6

Questions

Tap a question to see the answer.

Won't we lose protections for the neighborhood?

No. Town zoning, the noise and poultry ordinances, building and septic permits, and Act 250 all keep applying. Neighbors who want extra rules can sign a new, voluntary agreement among themselves.

Does this shut down the owners' association?

No. The association is a separate nonprofit corporation, and only its own board and members can dissolve it. Ending the rules removes the requirement that every owner join and pay dues. The association can keep meeting, keep its community seats, and keep sponsoring events with anyone who wants to take part.

I haven't paid dues. Can I still sign?

Yes. The rules give every owner one vote, and nothing ties it to dues.

Why not just fix the bad parts?

Only the developer's successor can propose changes (Article VI.B). Ending the rules is the one power the rules give owners directly.

Doesn't the association speak for us?

It has a seat on the board of Ascutney Outdoors and on the town's Utility Advisory Committee, and it sponsored the 2025 Brownsville July 4th events. That's real community work. A voluntary neighbors' group could keep doing all of it without recorded covenants or mandatory dues.

What if MFW objects?

The owners' vote ends the rules either way. Owners can also ask MFW to record a release of its rights.
